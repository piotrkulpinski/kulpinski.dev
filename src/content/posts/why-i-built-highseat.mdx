I run several sites that make money. [OpenAlternative](https://openalternative.co) alone brought in **$57,361** in [2025](/posts/2025-year-in-review). I used Plausible for a long time, and Stripe told me what people paid, but connecting revenue to its sources was always hard. I wanted to see both on one screen.

I also like trying analytics products, and I've always wanted to build one for myself.

[Highseat](https://highseat.com/?utm_source=kulpinski.dev&utm_medium=blog&utm_campaign=why-i-built-highseat) is what came out of that. Signups opened on 28 October, and the headline says what it's for: see which traffic makes money.

## What was missing

I've tried most of the analytics tools out there, and each one was missing something I needed: revenue attribution, deeper reports, better breakdowns or funnels.

I'm not the first person to have this idea, though. [Plausible](https://plausible.io) has funnels and revenue attribution on its Business plan, which stops at ten sites. [DataFast](https://datafa.st) has revenue by source on every plan, and it connects to seven payment providers against my two. If you sell through Paddle or Shopify, DataFast is the better fit today.

My bet is narrower: revenue on every breakdown, every feature on every plan, and one price however many sites you add.

## What I built

You connect Stripe or Polar with a webhook, and each sale lands next to the visit that made it. A refund comes off the sale it belongs to. Every page, source, country and device then shows what it earned, net of refunds. Credit goes to the last click by default, and you can switch to first click or linear at any time.

<Image src={revenue} alt="Highseat's homepage illustration of the Sources card: $31,240 gross, $1,946 in refunds and $29,294 net, with revenue and visitors for Direct, Google, DuckDuckGo, ChatGPT, Bing and Brave." />

The Bots report is the part I'd look at first. Plausible and Fathom filter data center traffic too, so the filtering isn't new. The report is the new part: it lists what Highseat kept out of your stats and the check that caught it, so you can see what was excluded instead of trusting a number.

On OpenAlternative's public dashboard, the last 7 days show 180.6k views and **151.4k bot hits kept out** (checked on 5 November). Crawlers are matched by user agent, not verified by IP, so that check has limits.

## How it got built

The first commit is from 21 November 2025, and it's a planning document. A proof of concept landed in March. That early work was only meant to prove I could get a first version running quickly, and to set up a base for the real product.

Then other projects needed more of my time, and I moved to them. A review in July found the proof of concept couldn't take events from a real site, and two of its five headline metrics were wrong or made up.

<ThemedImage light={commitsLight} dark={commitsDark} alt="Bar chart of commits per month on main: 6 in November 2025, almost none until 26 in July 2026, then 403 in September, 896 in October and 102 in the first five days of November." />

I only had time to come back in September. On the 1st I started again with a list of 15 features, ending with "probably some more I didn't think about". Revenue tracking was fifth. **1,401 of the 1,438 commits** on main came after that list, in 66 days. They carry my name, but AI agents did the coding.

Three weeks before signups opened, the marketing brief still said `{{PRODUCT}}` where the name should go. I was still looking for a good name and domain. Then I found highseat.com and liked it, but it was a premium domain. I negotiated it down to **$1,600**, which I think is a good deal for a domain like this.

## Three decisions and what they cost

### Deleting the salt

The Highseat tracker sets no cookies, and most sites that use it can drop the cookie banner. To count unique visitors without a cookie, it hashes a daily salt with the site, the IP and the user agent into a visitor id. The IP and user agent are never stored.

The first version derived the salt from a secret and the date. That meant any past day's salt could be recomputed, so every stored id stayed open to a brute force for as long as the secret lived. Now each day's salt is 32 random bytes, and it gets deleted 3.5 days after its day starts.

<ThemedImage light={saltLight} dark={saltDark} alt="Timeline of one salt's life: its day ends in the last time zone at 1.5 days, a session past midnight can use it until 2.5 days, a late event until 3.5 days, when the salt is deleted. Backups keep it up to 14 more days." />

It cost me two things. The daily database dump can't skip one table, so a deleted salt lives on in backups for up to 14 more days. An id becomes unlinkable within about 18 days, not 4. And a bot flood older than its salt can't be reclassified later by recomputing ids.

### One price axis, no free plan

A plan is one number: monthly events. Every plan has every feature, unlimited sites and unlimited seats, from $9 a month for 10k events to $549 for 50M. There's no overage fee, and a month only counts as over above 110% of the allowance. Instead of a free plan, there's a 14-day trial with no card.

The cost is that a cheaper tool exists below 100k events, and someone with ten small sites can fit them all on one small plan. A no-card trial also means a new account gets a new trial.

### Dropping first-party collection as the lead

On 1 September, the big bet was first-party collection: the script and events travel on your own domain. It led everything I wrote for seven weeks.

On 21 October I rewrote the brief. PostHog and Vercel give first-party delivery away, so it can't be the reason anyone pays. It's still in the product as a feature. But I threw out seven weeks of positioning, and I opened signups on a lead DataFast already claims.

## Where it stands

I don't know who Highseat is for yet. I tested four possible audiences on paper before opening signups, and none passed. In each one, the typical account pays $9 to $29 a month, and a cheaper rival exists. The first 20 to 30 paying customers will settle it.

It also has clear gaps. Revenue comes from Stripe and Polar only. Anonymous visitors can't be linked across days, so credit for a sale on a later day needs `identify()`. Imports cover Plausible and DataFast, and DataFast's revenue stays in DataFast. There's no Google Analytics import, no Search Console integration and no self-hosted edition.

Signups are open, but I haven't done an official launch yet, so I don't have much data. It's too early to say what I'd do differently.
